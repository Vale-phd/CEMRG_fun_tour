Welcome to Canterbury Cathedral, the spiritual heart of England for more than fourteen hundred years.

Its story begins in the year 597, when a monk named Augustine arrived from Rome, sent by Pope Gregory the Great to bring Christianity to the Anglo-Saxons. Augustine founded a church on this spot and became the very first Archbishop of Canterbury. Every archbishop since, more than a hundred of them, has sat upon the throne, the cathedra, that gives this building its name and makes it the senior cathedral in all of England. To this day the Archbishop of Canterbury leads the Church of England and is spiritual head to some eighty-five million Anglicans around the world.

The great building you see now rose mostly after a devastating fire in 1174. Look up at the soaring nave, rebuilt in the elegant Perpendicular Gothic style by the master mason Henry Yevele, and at the magnificent central tower, known as Bell Harry, finished around 1498, its delicate fan-vaulted ceiling floating more than seventy metres above the crossing.

But Canterbury's fame rests above all on one dramatic night. On the twenty-ninth of December, 1170, Archbishop Thomas Becket was struck down by four knights inside his own cathedral, after a bitter quarrel with King Henry the Second. The murder of an archbishop at his own altar shocked the whole of Christendom. Becket was declared a saint within three years, and his shrine became one of the greatest pilgrimage destinations in medieval Europe. These were the very pilgrims that Geoffrey Chaucer immortalised in The Canterbury Tales. Even the king himself came to do penance, walking barefoot through the streets to be scourged at the tomb.

The shrine was destroyed in 1538 on the orders of Henry the Eighth, and today a single lit candle marks the spot where it once blazed with gold and jewels. Around it lie kings and princes, among them Edward, the Black Prince, and King Henry the Fourth.

Recognised as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1988, Canterbury Cathedral is still a living, working church, where worship has continued, almost without pause, since the days of Augustine. As you step inside, remember that you are walking through fourteen centuries of English history.
