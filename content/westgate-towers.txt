Ahead of you rises the Westgate, the great twin-towered gateway that has guarded the western entrance to Canterbury for over six hundred years. It is the largest surviving medieval city gate in England, and the only one of Canterbury's gates still standing.

It was built around 1380 of grey Kentish ragstone, on the orders of Archbishop Simon Sudbury, and probably designed by Henry Yevele, the same master mason who rebuilt the nave of the cathedral. Sixty feet high, with two massive drum towers and a portcullis, it replaced an older gate on the road from London, the very road along which medieval pilgrims streamed towards Thomas Becket's shrine.

Look for the round holes beneath the windows. These are gun-loops, made for early cannon and handguns, which makes the Westgate one of the first fortifications in England designed for gunpowder weapons.

For most of its long life, though, the gate served a less warlike purpose, as the city gaol. For centuries prisoners were held in its towers, and you can still visit the cells and the condemned chamber inside.

The river Stour runs close by, and just beyond lie the peaceful Westgate Gardens. Stand here and imagine the noise of arriving pilgrims, traders, and travellers, all funnelling through this single gate into the medieval city, as you yourself are about to do.
