Ahead of you rise the Westgate, twin drum towers of grey ragstone, sixty feet high, the largest medieval city gate still standing in all of England, and the last survivor of Canterbury's seven gates.

Look closely at the stonework beneath the windows and you will spot small round holes. Those are gun-loops, openings made for early cannon and handguns, and they make the Westgate one of the very first buildings in England designed from the start for gunpowder weapons. It was raised around 1380, probably by Henry Yevele, the king's master mason, at a time when England feared a French invasion.

The gate was paid for in part by Archbishop Simon Sudbury, and there is a dark twist to his tale. As Lord Chancellor, Sudbury was blamed for the hated Poll Tax, and in the Peasants' Revolt of 1381 the mob dragged him from the Tower of London and beheaded him on Tower Hill.

For four centuries afterwards, these towers served as the city gaol, and you can still climb up to the old cells. Stand beneath the arch and picture the medieval crowds, pilgrims, traders, and drovers, all funnelling through this single gate into the city, exactly as you are about to do.
