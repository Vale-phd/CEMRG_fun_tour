Before you stand two bronze figures, King Ethelbert of Kent and his queen, Bertha, and between them lies the moment Christian England began.

Notice how they are turned towards one another, caught mid-conversation. The sculptor, Stephen Melton, set them here in 2006 to capture a scene from the year 597: Bertha returning from her prayers at Saint Martin's church, up the hill behind you, bringing her husband the news that a monk named Augustine has landed in Kent with a message from Rome.

Bertha was a Frankish princess, and already a Christian when she married the pagan Ethelbert. Her faith had quietly prepared the ground for everything that was to follow. And Ethelbert's own legacy runs deeper still. Around the year 603, he issued a written code of laws, the very first document ever written in the English language. Not a poem, not a prayer, but a plain list of fines and rules, and with it the English tongue steps for the first time into recorded history.

Stand a moment with this royal couple. From this quiet green, you might say, both English Christianity and English writing took their very first breath.
