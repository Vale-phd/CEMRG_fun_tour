Spread out before you are the ruins of Saint Augustine's Abbey, and though little now rises much above knee height, you are looking at one of the cradles of Christian England.

Trace the low walls and foundations across the grass, and you can read the footprint of an enormous church, one that once rivalled the cathedral itself. It was founded in 598 by Augustine, just a year after he arrived, as a Benedictine monastery and a worthy burial place for kings and archbishops. Here lie the earliest Archbishops of Canterbury, and here too were laid King Ethelbert of Kent, the first English king to accept Christianity, and his queen, Bertha, whose faith had quietly opened the door.

For nearly a thousand years the abbey was one of the great centres of learning in the kingdom, its monks copying manuscripts, teaching, and praying on this very ground. Then, in 1538, Henry the Eighth dissolved the monasteries. The great church was pulled down and its stone carted away for other buildings, leaving only the foundations you see today, now in the care of English Heritage.

With the cathedral and nearby Saint Martin's, the abbey forms Canterbury's World Heritage Site, the three places where, fourteen centuries ago, English Christianity was born.
