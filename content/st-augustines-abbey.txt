Before you lie the ruins of St Augustine's Abbey, and though little now stands much above waist height, this is one of the most important sites in English history.

It was founded in 598, just a year after Augustine arrived in Canterbury, as a Benedictine monastery and a burial place fit for archbishops and kings. Here lie the graves of the earliest Archbishops of Canterbury, and of King Aethelberht of Kent, the first English king to accept Christianity, together with his queen, Bertha. It was Bertha, a Christian Frankish princess, whose faith had quietly prepared the ground for Augustine's mission.

For nearly a thousand years the abbey flourished as one of the great centres of learning and worship in the kingdom, its church rivalling the cathedral itself in size and splendour. Monks copied manuscripts, taught, and prayed here, keeping the flame of scholarship alive through the early Middle Ages.

That long story ended abruptly in 1538, when Henry the Eighth dissolved the monasteries. The abbey was stripped, its great church pulled down, and its stones carried away for other buildings. What you see today are the foundations and fragments left behind, cared for now by English Heritage.

Together with the cathedral and nearby St Martin's Church, St Augustine's Abbey forms part of Canterbury's UNESCO World Heritage Site, the three places where, fourteen centuries ago, the Christianity of England was born.
