This is the boarding point for a trip along the Great Stour, and from the water, Canterbury shows you a face the streets never do.

Look at the riverbanks, where ancient buildings lean right out over the water, their timber frames bowed with age and their gardens tumbling down to the edge. Just downstream stands the Old Weavers' House, a beautiful Tudor building that became home to Huguenot and Walloon weavers, Protestant refugees from France and the Low Countries who fled to Canterbury in the sixteenth century and brought their cloth-making skills with them.

The Stour has always been the city's working artery. Its waters once drove fulling mills and corn mills, carried goods, and supplied the great religious houses. The friars even built right across it. The guides who row you along these days will point out the old ducking stool, the medieval stonework, and the places where the river splits and rejoins around the heart of the city.

So step aboard, mind the low bridges, and keep an eye out for the swans. Gliding beneath the willows, you will see eight hundred years of Canterbury reflected in the water, a slower, greener, altogether lovelier view of the city.
