This striking modern building on the riverside is the Marlowe Theatre, and it carries the name of Canterbury's most dazzling literary son.

Look up at the great windowless tower rising from the centre. That is the fly tower, where scenery is hoisted high out of sight above the stage, and the architect Keith Williams made it the building's bold landmark when the new theatre opened in 2011, here on the banks of the Stour. An older cinema turned theatre stood on this spot before it, but this one was built from scratch to give the city a stage worthy of its name.

And what a name. Christopher Marlowe was baptised here in Canterbury in 1564, just two months before a certain William Shakespeare was born in Stratford. Marlowe blazed brilliantly and briefly. He gave the English stage its first truly soaring verse, in plays like Doctor Faustus, dabbled in spying and scandal, and was killed in a tavern in Deptford at just twenty-nine, stabbed above the eye in a quarrel said to be over the bill.

Tonight, behind that fly tower, the lights will dim and the curtain will rise. The city is still telling stories, four and a half centuries after its greatest storyteller was born.
