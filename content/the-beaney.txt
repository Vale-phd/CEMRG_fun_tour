This handsome black-and-white building on the High Street is the Beaney House of Art and Knowledge, Canterbury's museum, gallery, and library, all under one mock-Tudor roof.

Look up at that timber-framed front. It looks every inch a Tudor building, but it was actually built in 1899, and behind its old-fashioned face lies a wonderfully mixed collection: paintings and pottery, beetles and butterflies, ancient curiosities, and the occasional stuffed surprise.

The story behind it is rather wonderful. James George Beaney was born here in Canterbury, the son of a poor labourer. He trained as a surgeon, sailed to Australia, and made both his name and his fortune as a doctor in Melbourne. When he died, he remembered the city of his birth and the working men of his own humble background, and he left ten thousand pounds, a small fortune in those days, to build an institute where ordinary people could better themselves through art and learning. Knowledge, he believed, should be free to everyone.

And it still is. Step inside, and it is all free, the galleries, the library, the lot. So go in, wander the cabinets, and enjoy the gift that a labourer's son sent home to Canterbury from the far side of the world.
