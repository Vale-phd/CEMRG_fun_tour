You are standing before Fyndon's Gate, also known as the Great Gate of St Augustine's Abbey, one of the most magnificent medieval gatehouses in England.

It was built between 1300 and 1309 by Abbot Thomas Fyndon, whose name it still carries, as the grand ceremonial entrance to the abbey. Faced with flint and pale stone, flanked by tall octagonal turrets and crowned with battlements, it was designed to impress every pilgrim, bishop, and king who passed beneath it.

And kings certainly did. The fine chamber above the archway became royal lodgings. In June 1625, the newly crowned Charles the First waited here to receive his young bride, Henrietta Maria of France, and the couple are said to have spent their first nights together within these walls. Other monarchs and great visitors had been welcomed through this gate before them.

When Henry the Eighth dissolved the abbey in 1538, much of it was torn down, but the gate was too useful and too splendid to lose, and it was kept as the entrance to a royal residence raised among the ruins. Over the centuries storms and time took their toll, and the upper levels were carefully restored.

Today Fyndon's Gate belongs to the King's School, and pupils pass beneath its seven-hundred-year-old arch every day. Pause for a moment and look up at the carved stonework and the great wooden doors. This is the threshold to the very birthplace of English Christianity, which lies just beyond.
