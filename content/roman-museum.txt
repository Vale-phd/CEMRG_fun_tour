Beneath the modern street, down a flight of steps, lies a genuine Roman house, and the Roman Museum is built right around it, exactly where it was found.

Look down at the mosaic floor. Those thousands of tiny coloured stones were laid around the year 300, in the corridor of a grand townhouse in the Roman city of Durovernum. Beside it you can see the remains of a hypocaust, the Roman underfloor heating, where hot air from a furnace once circulated beneath the floor on little brick pillars. Central heating, in Britain, eighteen centuries ago, a comfort that vanished from these islands for over a thousand years after the Romans left.

And here is the extraordinary part: this house was lost for centuries, and only rediscovered through disaster. On the night of the thirty-first of May, 1942, German bombers struck Canterbury in the so-called Baedeker raids, named after the tourist guidebook the enemy used to pick out England's loveliest cities. When the rubble was finally cleared, the bombs had laid bare this Roman pavement, hidden beneath the city for sixteen hundred years.

Stand here over the mosaic and look down through the layers, modern shops above, a Roman dining room below. In Canterbury, the past is quite literally beneath your feet.
