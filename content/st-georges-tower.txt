This lone tower, marooned among the modern shops, is all that remains of the church of Saint George the Martyr, and it guards a remarkable memory.

In 1564, at the font of this church, a baby was christened: Christopher Marlowe, a shoemaker's son, who would grow up to become the most brilliant playwright of his age, until a young man from Stratford named Shakespeare came to eclipse him. Marlowe was baptised here on the twenty-sixth of February, and his family lived just across the street.

Then came the night of the thirty-first of May, 1942. German bombers struck the city in the Baedeker raids, and Saint George's was gutted by fire, burned down to a roofless shell. The clock on the tower, look up, and you can still see its face, stopped that night at eighteen minutes past two. The ruined church was finally cleared away in the 1950s, but the tower was spared, left standing as a memorial to all that was lost.

So pause beneath it, amid the bustle of the high street. This single tower has witnessed the baptism of a genius and the burning of a city, and still it keeps its silent watch today, a quiet survivor among the shopfronts.
